We met on a grey afternoon to talk about the impossible thing translators do every day: carry a voice out of one language and set it down, more or less intact, in another. What follows has been edited for length and clarity.

You've said translation is less a science than a series of apologies. What do you mean by that?

Every choice loses something. You gain a rhyme and lose a rhythm; you keep the sense and let the music go. The honest translator is always apologising to one language for what had to be given up to please the other. You learn to make your peace with that, or you find another line of work.

A translation is not a copy. It is a performance of a text — and like any performance, it bears the marks of the person giving it.

Is there a word you've never managed to move across?

Several. There is always one that sits in the original like a stone in a shoe — perfectly at home there, impossible here. When that happens I stop fighting it. I let the footnote do the apologising, and I try to make the surrounding sentences generous enough that the reader forgives the gap.

What keeps you at it?

The moment — rare, but it comes — when a line arrives in the new language as if it had always meant to be there. You did not invent it and you did not merely copy it. You were, for a sentence, the place where two languages agreed. That is worth a great deal of apologising.
