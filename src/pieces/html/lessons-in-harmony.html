What leads Jonathan Coe, a renowned British state-of-the-nation comic novelist, to write a work of historical fiction narrated by a Greek-Spanish music critic who befriends a French composer and his English pupil?

Coe achieved fame with 1994’s What a Carve Up!, his comic-gothic takedown of Thatcherism, and a trilogy of British political satires starting with 2001’s The Rotters’ Club. But his full body of work, now numbering some 16 novels to date, displays a greater eclecticism of genre and subject matter. This new work more closely recalls Expo 58, Coe’s 2013 comedy set in 1950s Brussels and riffing on the tropes and clichés of the “Englishman abroad”, and Mr Wilder and Me, his 2020 novel based on real historical characters and exploring themes of creativity, age and friendship.

Lessons in Harmony charts the real-life friendship between Ralph Vaughan Williams and Maurice Ravel. In 1907, the 35-year-old Vaughan Williams went to Paris to study under the 32-year-old Ravel. Thereafter, Vaughan Williams, in his own words, threw off “the heavy contrapuntal Teutonic manner” on his way to becoming a national treasure. Ravel inspired him without unduly influencing him, and famously said that Vaughan Williams was his only pupil “who does not write my music”. Beyond those facts, their relationship is only sketchily documented, which gives Coe the freedom to flesh out the story in his own witty and suggestive ways.

The novel comes with an elaborate framing device. Lessons in Harmony is ostensibly the manuscript of a memoir written by the fictional character “PV Mylonas” (based on a friend of Ravel’s who had introduced the two composers), written in 1943 and discovered decades later. To make matters tricksier, it is presented as if edited by the equally fictional “Justine La Chasse”, dizzyingly self-described as the granddaughter of the “housekeeper to the widower of the masseuse of Ravel’s younger brother”.

Anyone braced for postmodern narrative surprises will wait in vain. The manuscript really does seem to be little more than an elaborate scaffold to allow for Coe’s scholarly footnotes. Mylonas himself is a delightful narrator, with a gift for campy fastidiousness: at one point, the repeated phrase “words fail me” encloses a page-long paragraph of fluent outrage.

The novel is a collection of incidents: lessons, meals, concerts, meetings. Coe has done his research on everything from long-gone London pubs to the titles of burlesque shows, and delivers a reassuringly plausible Paris before the first world war. Throughout, he stays largely faithful to the historical record.  

In doing so, he necessarily forgoes the elaborate plotting of some of his other work, and the book’s biggest “revelation” is a fact obvious to the reader long before it can be admitted by the narrator. But it is very funny throughout, and very touching. Coe is, as always, skilled at portraying relationships that don’t quite work: clumsy affections, unrealistic yearnings, dutiful marriages. The complex Vaughan Williams marriage, with its fierce loyalties and private anguish, is particularly well drawn.

Coe is also a passionate music enthusiast, who has dabbled in composing and performing himself. Ravel is one of his favourite composers, and the novel, which comes with a Spotify playlist, is fascinating on the music it discusses.

Emotional traffic and interesting exchanges about music, nationhood and love are what drives Lessons in Harmony. It begins with Vaughan Williams wanting to be “a sort of national composer, if you like”; and by the end Ravel describes him, affectionately but not uncomplicatedly, as “vraiment un compositeur national”. Across the whole novel, though, given the little matter of two world wars, the troubling question of what national culture might mean has been diligently worked over.

The cross-Channel set-up allows for a lot of highly enjoyable comic set pieces, and much joshing about national stereotypes, in both directions. But it also offers a sense of fraught possibility that might resonate for the modern reader: “a fusion of two personalities – but much more than that, two nations, even … something entirely new and beautiful, something that was far greater than the sum of its parts”.

There is also a multi-faceted consideration of politics and art, with a curmudgeonly Gustav Holst, for example, championing the working-class authenticity of the music of Vaughan Williams, and disparaging what he takes to be the apolitical fripperies of Ravel. But it is Ravel who turns down the Légion d’Honneur for political reasons, and Vaughan Williams who comes from the privileged classes.

Likewise, Coe has Erik Satie tease Vaughan Williams about his folk-song collecting. Peasant farmers want to go home after a hard day’s toil in the fields, he says. “Now they have to dredge up ancient tunes from their memory and provide this well-dressed gentleman with raw materials for his string quartets and his symphonies.” And years later Vaughan Williams himself muses, wryly: “what can one really do, as a composer? … Oh, it’s all very well offering statements. What difference does it make? I mean, here we are … It’s almost as if Herr Hitler has never attended one of my concerts.”

Lessons in Harmony is a delicious composition: entertaining, poignant and covertly argumentative. Its ideas appear as motifs, answering each other subtly, while human feeling, however restrained or constrained, remains at its centre.

In both its form and its themes, the book can also be read as a disguised meditation on Coe’s own aesthetics. If, in the past, he has been accused of a fondness for artifice, here we have Ravel, the son of an engineer, revelling in that very charge. And while the subject of Englishness has at times been emotionally conflicting for Coe, in this novel he sketches “a more generous patriotism – hospitable and outward-looking, ironic and self-doubting”. Ultimately, Lessons in Harmony achieves in language the pleasure, understatement, delicacy and emotional weight which its own characters seek to deliver in music.
