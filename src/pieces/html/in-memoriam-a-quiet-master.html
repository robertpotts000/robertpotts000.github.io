The writers we lose loudly are easy to mourn; the quiet ones ask more of us. This was a writer who distrusted the grand gesture, who cut every sentence that raised its voice, and who built, out of that lifelong restraint, a body of work that will outlast a good deal of what shouted past it.

There were no manifestos. There was instead a patient attention to small things — a hand on a table, the light in a provincial railway station, the exact temperature of a conversation between two people who no longer quite understand each other. From such materials whole worlds were made, and never once did the making draw attention to itself.

To say little, and to mean all of it: that was the whole art, and it is harder than it looks.

Colleagues remember a courtesy that never curdled into blandness, and a critical honesty that never hardened into cruelty. Younger writers remember letters — always handwritten, always shorter than hoped, and always containing the one sentence that changed how they saw their own work.

We are left with the books, which is to say we are left with the best part. They will go on doing quietly what their author did: waiting, without insistence, for the reader ready to be still enough to hear them.
