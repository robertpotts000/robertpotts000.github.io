Somewhere in the last few decades the clean sentence won. Short, declarative, scrubbed of subordinate clauses, it became the house style of the age — the prose equivalent of good posture. We are told to cut, to prune, to let the verbs do the work. Much of this advice is sound. Some of it has quietly cost us something.

A sentence is not only a delivery system for information. It is also a shape in time, a way of moving a reader's attention from one thing to the next, and the long sentence — the one that gathers, qualifies, doubles back — can hold in suspension what the clean sentence must break into pieces.

What the clause knows

The subordinate clause is where thought admits its own complications. It is the place a writer says "and yet," "although," "which is to say" — the small hinges on which honest prose turns. Strip them all away and you are left with a series of confident assertions that no longer sound quite like thinking.

Clarity is a virtue. It is not the only virtue, and it is not the same thing as truth.

This is not a plea for difficulty for its own sake, nor a defence of the baggy and the vague. It is only a reminder that style is a set of choices, and that the unruly clause — used well, used deliberately — remains one of them. The clean sentence is a fine tool. It should not be the only one in the box.
