There is a kind of poem that will not sit still to be explained. You approach it expecting a handhold and find instead a surface that gives nothing back, and the temptation — always — is to call this failure. It is easier to say a poem is obscure than to admit we have not yet learned how to read it.

But difficulty is not the same as evasion. The best of it is a form of respect: an assumption that the reader is willing to be slowed down, to sit inside a sentence that does not resolve on first contact. What looks like withholding is often only patience, asking for patience in return.

The clear poem tells you what it means. The difficult poem trusts you to find out — and changes you in the finding.

The reader's labour

Consider how much of the pleasure of a hard poem lives in the work itself. We do not admire a puzzle for its answer but for the shape of the thinking it demands. A line that yields at once is soon forgotten; a line that resists lodges in the mind and keeps working there, long after the book is closed.

None of this is an argument for wilful murk. There is bad difficulty — the kind that hides an absence of feeling behind a screen of cleverness — and part of the critic's job is to tell the two apart. But we should be slow to reach for the word "pretentious," which is so often just a way of refusing the labour a poem asks of us.

To read well is to be willing, now and then, to be defeated. The poem that finally opens does so on its own terms, and the reward is not comprehension so much as company: the sense of a mind meeting yours across the distance the difficulty made.
